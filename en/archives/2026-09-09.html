The question at hand

Metropolises vote left, small towns and rural France swing right: can the country still move forward as one?

The facts

On the evening of 22 March 2026, after the second round of the municipal elections, the French electoral map confirmed a split in two. In the big metropolises, nothing really changed: the Socialist Party and its allies held on to Paris, Marseille, Lille, Nantes, and Rennes. The National Rally, for its part, remains confined to a single city of over 100,000 residents, Perpignan — a glass ceiling* that has held through several elections. But in towns under 30,000 residents — especially the Pas-de-Calais mining basin and the whole Mediterranean arc — the RN built continuous territorial blocs. National tally: 57 town halls won, versus just 9 in 2020 — a sixfold rise in a single term. The number of RN town councillors, meanwhile, jumped from 827 to 3,121, with elected officials now present, for the first time, in 84 of France's 101 départements.

Understanding it

What these results confirm, a geographer had already announced back in 2014: France is splitting between its globalized metropolises and its periphery.

In 2014, geographer Christophe Guilluy published La France périphérique ("Peripheral France")*. His thesis, contested at the time: big cities concentrate globalization's winners, while the rural and suburban periphery inherits the working classes left behind, far from skilled jobs. Twelve years later, the ballots of March 2026 draw almost exactly that map.

Between these two extremes, mid-sized towns (30,000 to 100,000 residents) saw the RN's most visible gains — La Seyne-sur-Mer, Montauban, Cagnes-sur-Mer. The Greens, for their part, collapsed: 8 of their 12 major cities lost, holding on only to Lyon and Tours, and only just. La France Insoumise, meanwhile, took hold in about ten working-class suburban towns, Saint-Denis and Vénissieux chief among them. Another sign of the fracture: turnout climbed back to 57.03% in the national second round, up from 41.86% in 2020 in the middle of the Covid crisis, but still well below the 62.13% of 2014. It nonetheless fell by 11 points in towns under 1,000 residents, while big cities edged up slightly.

This electoral fracture rests on very real ground, measured figure after figure by INSEE and the Court of Auditors across three Frances that look less and less alike:

Three Frances, one country
  • 1 The metropolises 67% of the population (43 million people) · population growth +0.4%/year · Paris, Marseille, Lille, Nantes, Rennes loyal to the left and center · Île-de-France alone: 31% of national GDP.
  • 2 Mid-sized towns and the suburban fringe 20% of the population, rural areas under urban influence · the RN's most notable gains in 2026 (La Seyne-sur-Mer, Montauban, Cagnes-sur-Mer) · often 45 minutes from the nearest hospital.
  • 3 Deep rural France 14% of the population · growth twice as slow (+0.2%/year) · 23 départements in demographic decline, versus about a dozen in the 1980s-1990s · RN entrenched in continuous blocs (Pas-de-Calais, Mediterranean arc).

Three Frances that share the same currency, the same laws, and the same Parliament — less and less the same daily life.

Here's the paradox: the French have never been so connected to one another — same platforms, same currency, same news channels — and yet their zip code weighs more and more on their vote, their access to a doctor or a school, and their share of the country's wealth. GDP per capita sums it up on its own: €68,390 in Paris, versus €35,640 in Lyon and €32,260 in Marseille — a two-to-one gap between big cities themselves. Riding this rural and suburban base, the National Rally is now credited with roughly 35% of first-round voting intentions for the 2027 presidential election, up from 33% at the start of the year (Ipsos, Cluster17, IFOP-Fiducial, late-summer 2026 polls). We already looked at who could actually beat the RN in the runoff: this territorial fracture is one of its underlying drivers.

What happens next largely hinges on very concrete budget choices. In June, the Senate passed a watered-down version of a bill against medical deserts*, without any strong constraint on where doctors can set up practice. The France Ruralités* plan, for its part, is due to be renewed — or not — in the 2027 budget, to be debated this fall — a budget we already showed the Lecornu government might not survive passing. These two files carry much of the sense of abandonment that keeps feeding, figure after figure, the country's metropolisation*.

A real catch-up plan narrows the territorial fracture

Parliament follows through on a real regulation of where doctors can practice, beyond the watered-down version passed by the Senate in June, and the 3,700 junior doctors expected from November 2026 are actually deployed to underserved areas. The 2027 budget protects, or even boosts, funding for the France Ruralités plan and decentralization, with real financial transfers to rural towns and mid-sized cities. The sense of abandonment recedes in these areas, without erasing years of accumulated lag.

This is the least likely of the three scenarios, because it requires the Lecornu government, already fragile on the budget, to win a majority for measures stricter than the ones already watered down in the Senate. Less likely than stable, which requires no new political choice. Also less likely than degraded, whose cause — pressure on public finances — remains very real.

The gap neither closes nor widens

Nearly one in three French people already lives in an area where access to a doctor is considered degraded, and 6.4 million have no regular physician. The bill against medical deserts stays in its watered-down version passed by the Senate in June, with no strong constraint on where doctors set up practice. Existing measures — installation grants, mobile health buses, telehealth, the arrival of 3,700 junior doctors — keep going at the same pace, without closing the gap. The 2027 budget keeps France Ruralités funding roughly at its current level, neither boosting nor drastically cutting it. The National Rally keeps consolidating its hold on the areas it has already won, without a dramatic breakthrough elsewhere.

This is the most likely of the three scenarios, because it simply extends the trajectory of recent years: no budget breakthrough, no further rupture. More likely than favorable, which would require a political choice the Lecornu government has no means to force through. Also more likely than degraded, since no drastic cuts are currently on the table in the budget debate.

The fracture deepens further

Under pressure from the deficit and the debt, the 2027 budget cuts funding for the France Ruralités plan and decentralization. The bill against medical deserts, already watered down in the Senate, is shelved or gutted in the National Assembly. School and public-service closures accelerate in départements already in decline, while wealth keeps concentrating in Île-de-France and the big metropolises. The sense of abandonment worsens across peripheral France.

This scenario remains less likely than stable, since it requires a vote for outright budget cuts in an already tense climate. But it is more likely than favorable, because its cause — pressure from the public deficit on already tight trade-offs — is structural, not cyclical, and a budget crisis this fall would be enough to make it worse still.

Key takeaways

Big cities and peripheral France vote, live, and age in increasingly different ways: can the country still move forward as one whole?

In the March 2026 municipal elections, the National Rally went from 9 to 57 town halls while France's big metropolises barely felt any effect — an electoral glass ceiling that mirrors a very real wealth gap: Île-de-France alone accounts for 31% of national GDP.

The most likely outcome (45%): neither catch-up nor further worsening — current measures against medical deserts and for rural areas continue at the same pace without closing the gap, while the National Rally consolidates its hold on the areas it has already won.

Signal to watch: the content of the 2027 budget for local governments and the France Ruralités plan, to be debated this fall, and how RN polling for the presidential race evolves as spring 2027 approaches.

Fairly negative

Our assessment of the impact for France: fairly negative. The stable scenario, the most likely (45%), keeps an already-existing fracture in place without closing it; the degraded scenario (35%) would make it worse still. Together, these two scenarios carry an 80% probability, against just 20% for a genuine catch-up.

Quick glossary

France périphérique (Peripheral France)
A concept coined in 2014 by geographer Christophe Guilluy to describe the rural areas, suburbs, and small towns far from big metropolises, where the working classes hit hardest by globalization are said to be concentrated.
Glass ceiling (electoral)
An invisible limit that keeps a party from exceeding a certain level of results in a given type of area — here, big cities — despite sharply rising scores elsewhere.
Medical desert
An area where the supply of healthcare is clearly insufficient relative to the population's needs, forcing people to travel far to see a doctor.
France Ruralités
A government plan launched in 2023 to support rural areas (access to healthcare, public services, housing), regularly expanded since.
Metropolisation
The growing concentration of population, skilled jobs, and wealth in big cities, at the expense of mid-sized towns and rural areas.
